# 02. Parent review: copy deck (opening, cross-links, view-count source line)

Reviewer role: Parent. I have a twelve-year-old who games a lot. The "13.7 years younger" post got forwarded to me. I am reading on a phone and I am a little wary of a gaming coaching company explaining a gaming study to me.

Scope: comprehension and trust only. I did not see code, specs or the company's reasoning. I do not rewrite copy. The two optional audience files named in my brief do not exist, so I read from the persona alone.

Ratings: COMPREHENSION or TRUST, then BLOCKER / SHOULD FIX / NOTE.

---

## Verdict in short

- Paragraph 2, Version B reads better on first pass.
- One BLOCKER: the subhead's "More than 30 million people saw it" does not match the article's own source line.
- Paragraph 1 is the strongest thing here. "It tells you nothing about your twelve-year-old" is the sentence that made me decide to keep reading a company's blog.
- Of the five cross-links, (1) and (3) feel like pointers, (2) feels like an ad, (4) and (5) made me stumble.

---

## PART 1. The opening

### What I took from the title, subhead and paragraph 1 (same in both versions)

A doctor posted one sentence about a gaming study and it went everywhere. The study was 923 adults around age 55. Gamers scored better on some thinking tests. Nobody can say gaming caused it, and there were no kids in it, so it says nothing about my son. The famous number came from a press release, not the study. The writer thinks what matters for a kid is what he plays and who with.

That is a clear, honest answer, and I got it in one paragraph. Good.

### Version A, in my own words

The press release line got to the writer through a post on X, same as it got to me. A doctor with a lot of followers shared the study summary with "the one sentence" on top. It got 30 million views in a week if you count the reposts. People argued for three days.

What the article will tell me: why the number is wrong and what people said in the replies.

### Version B, in my own words

The writer saw a post on X like I did. A doctor with a lot of followers posted a study summary and one sentence, that frequent gamers tested like non-gamers 13.7 years younger. It got 30 million views in a week with the reposts. People argued for three days.

What the article will tell me: the same thing, why the number is wrong and what people said in the replies.

### Which is clearer

**Version B.** It tells me what the sentence was. Version A makes me go back up to paragraph 1 to work out what "That press-release line" and "the one sentence" are.

---

### Findings

**F1. TRUST. BLOCKER. The subhead's number does not match the source line.**
Subhead: "A doctor posted one line about a study. More than 30 million people saw it."
Source line: "original post 17.2M".
"It" in the subhead is the doctor's post. The doctor's post is at 17.2 million. The 30 million only works if you add in other people's posts that quoted it, which paragraph 2 says but the subhead does not. Also, views are not people. The same person scrolling past three times is three views.
Why I rate this a blocker: the whole article is "the number everyone repeated is not in the source." If the writer's own headline number is not in his own source line, he is doing the thing he is calling out. I would only catch it if I scrolled to the sources, but if I did, I would stop trusting the rest.

**F2. COMPREHENSION. SHOULD FIX. "That press-release line" (Version A) only works if I read paragraph 1 slowly.**
Does it make sense at that point? Yes, but only just. I had to look back up. On a phone a lot of us skim a paragraph that opens "Short answer:" and jump to where the story starts. If I did that, "That press-release line" points at nothing.
There is a second snag. Version A says the doctor "shared the study's abstract with the one sentence on top." So was the sentence from the press release, or from the study? For a second I thought the doctor was quoting the study. Version A never says what "the one sentence" was.

**F3. COMPREHENSION. SHOULD FIX. "counted gaming differently" (paragraph 1).**
I stumbled here. Differently from what? Differently how? It is the one fuzzy phrase in an otherwise sharp paragraph, and it is the part that explains where the famous number came from. I am left knowing the number is "not in the paper" but not really why.

**F4. COMPREHENSION. SHOULD FIX. "a recent post" will not hold up.**
Three months from now "recent" is wrong, and I will notice. There is also no date for the post anywhere in the opening. The only dates I see are "2024 press release" and "Updated October 12, 2026." That left me unsure whether this is news from this month or an old thing going around again. A month and year for the post would settle it.

**F5. TRUST. SHOULD FIX. The study itself is not named or linked where the claim is made.**
Paragraph 1 says "one study of 923 adults." Which study, by whom? The only link in the opening goes to X. I do not have an X account and many parents do not, so that link may hit a login wall. The thing I actually want to check is the study and the press release. If those links are only at the bottom, I am taking the short answer on faith from a company that sells gaming coaching.

**F6. TRUST. NOTE. "My first reaction was "duh.""**
This is where my guard came up. Paragraph 1 just told me the study cannot say gaming caused anything. Then the founder of a gaming company says his gut reaction to "games make you smart" was "duh." My reaction: of course you would say that.
What saves it is the very next sentence, where he says the number is not in the paper. And the heading "Why "duh"" tells me he is about to explain. So I kept reading. But it is a wobble, and it is not clear in that moment what he is saying "duh" to.

**F7. COMPREHENSION. NOTE. Repetition.**
Does it annoy me? Not quite, but I noticed.
- "The number is not in the paper" reaches me three times before the first heading: the tape in the hero image, paragraph 1, and paragraph 3. By paragraph 3 it is written as if it is a reveal ("after I read the paper...") but I already know.
- The reach of the post comes up twice: subhead and paragraph 2. That is fine.
- The replies come up twice: subhead and paragraph 3. That is fine, it is the hook.
Version A makes the repetition a little worse, because "That press-release line" tells me the punchline again one paragraph before paragraph 3 delivers it.

**F8. COMPREHENSION. NOTE. Version B wording.**
"With the posts that quoted it, it passed 30 million views" is a bit loose. Version A's "Counting the posts that quoted it" told me more plainly that the number is a total. That is the one place Version A was clearer.
"probably the same way you did" is slightly off for me. I did not see it on X. My kid sent it to me as proof. Small thing.

**F9. TRUST. NOTE. Title promise versus the short answer.**
The title says "what it means for your twelve-year-old." Paragraph 1 says "it tells you nothing about your twelve-year-old." I read that as honest and I liked it. But it does leave me asking why I should read on. The last sentence of paragraph 1 ("what kind of gaming, and who he plays with") is the answer, and it is also the first moment I can hear the company's product in the background. It is fair as an opinion because he says "I think."

**F10. COMPREHENSION. NOTE. Small things.**
- "who he plays with." My kid is a boy, so it fits me. A parent of a girl will feel this is not about her.
- "three or more hours a week" next to the image saying "Frequent video gamers." My son plays three hours on a Saturday morning. That gap is interesting and useful to me, and nobody points at it.
- "A doctor" in the subhead and image, "A physician" in paragraph 2. Same person, I assume.
- "Updated October 12, 2026" with no first-published date. Updated from when, and what changed?
- The man in the headset in the hero image is not identified. I assume it is the writer.
- The sticky note "BETTER THAN SPORTS?" is exactly what I would ask. I now expect the article to answer it.
- "I read a thousand of the replies." My first thought was "really?" The cross-links say "about a thousand," which I believe more.

**F11. TRUST. NOTE. A link to Elon Musk's quote-post: neither, leaning slightly less.**
I do not doubt that the post was big. It reached me. So proof of view counts does not buy any trust from me. What I doubt is the science and the motive of the company telling me about it.
Putting that name near the top would pull my attention to him and away from my kid. Some parents will like seeing it and some will be put off, and either way it is a distraction. If it is in the sources list at the bottom as a place to check the counts, fine. In the opening, no.
What would raise my trust is a link to the study and the press release right where paragraph 1 makes its claims (see F5).

**Does any sentence make me trust the writer less?** Only two moments: the subhead number (F1) and "duh" (F6). Everything in paragraph 1 made me trust him more.

## PART 2. The five cross-link sentences

On the question asked for all five: does "the number isn't in the paper" make sense if I have not read the post? **Mostly yes.** I understand "the paper" as "the study," and it makes me ask "so where did it come from?", which is a reason to click. Two cautions:
- It can sound like "the study is fake." Then links (2) and (5) go on to use the replies to that same study to back up the company's view. That reads a bit like having it both ways.
- It works best in (3), where it is paired with "and the study was of adults." That pairing tells me the two things I need.

### (1) End of the founder's essay

- Do I know what I get? Yes. An explanation of the number and what it means for a kid my son's age.
- Pointer or ad? Pointer.
- **L1. COMPREHENSION. SHOULD FIX.** "If the viral "13.7 years younger" gaming study is why you're here" comes at the end of a different essay. If the study was why I came, I would not have just finished reading something else. The "if" feels misplaced, and it tells me the pointer I needed was at the bottom of the page.
- NOTE: "what it means for a twelve-year-old." Fits me exactly. A parent of a nine-year-old or a fifteen-year-old may think it is not for them.

### (2) End of the League of Legends article

- Do I know what I get? Roughly. "I wrote up what is" made me stop and reread. What is what? I worked out it means "what is in the paper."
- Pointer or ad? **Ad.** This is the one that feels like selling.
- **L2. TRUST. SHOULD FIX.** The page is the company explaining why it coaches a particular game. Then a stranger's reply on X is held up as "this whole page in eleven words." The most-liked reply under a viral post is a popular opinion, not evidence. Using it to pat the company's own choice on the back made me trust the page a little less, not more.
- **L3. COMPREHENSION. SHOULD FIX.** It is long and does three jobs at once: tells a story, quotes a reply, corrects a number. "The 13.7 isn't in the paper. I wrote up what is" is the part I tripped on.
- NOTE: "I wrote up." If the League article is not signed by the same person, I do not know who "I" is.

### (3) Note after the sources of the guest mom's article

- Do I know what I get? **No.** It ends "EKUZO founder Karlin Oei read about a thousand of those replies." And then what? It never says he wrote something, or that there is an answer to the question the parents asked.
- Pointer or ad? Pointer. "Related:" is an honest label, and the question in it ("how to get a kid off the game without the attitude") is my actual life.
- **L4. COMPREHENSION. SHOULD FIX.** The sentence raises my exact question and then stops without telling me whether clicking gets me an answer. That is the best hook of the five and it has no payoff.
- NOTE: A company note sitting under a guest mom's article could feel like the company stepping into her piece. Being placed after the sources and labeled "Related" keeps it on the right side for me.
- NOTE: The parenthesis is carrying a lot, but I followed it.

### (4) Near the end of the homeschool parents article

- Do I know what I get? Partly. Another article of the same type, about the study replies. It does not say what is in it for my kid.
- Pointer or ad? Pointer.
- **L5. COMPREHENSION. SHOULD FIX.** "the replies sorted into camps there too." This company sells camps. It is in the menu. For a beat I read "camps" as the product. I know what was meant, but that word on this site is a trip hazard.
- **L6. COMPREHENSION. SHOULD FIX.** "I ran the same read." I do not talk like that. I had to translate it to "I did the same kind of analysis."
- NOTE: "This piece has a sibling" is friendly. It does lean on me having absorbed how the homeschool article was put together.

### (5) Inside the article about boys whose only friends are online

- Do I know what I get? **Not really.** I had to read it twice.
- Pointer or ad? Closer to an interruption than either.
- **L7. COMPREHENSION. SHOULD FIX.** "The same idea surfaced." Which idea? The sentence before it is "The answers tell you whether to worry, and what to do about it." That is not an idea a reply could echo. I am judging this from the one sentence of context I was given, so it may read better in place. From here, "the same idea" points at nothing.
- **L8. COMPREHENSION. SHOULD FIX.** The structure is hard on a phone: a parenthesis with a semicolon inside it, in the middle of a sentence that then has a colon and a quote. By the time I got to the quote I had lost the start.
- **L9. TRUST. SHOULD FIX.** Placement. If I am reading about a boy whose only friends are online, I am probably worried about my own. The article has just promised to tell me "whether to worry, and what to do about it." Pausing there to mention a viral study and the founder's reading project feels like the company talking about itself at the moment I most want it to talk about my son. The other four are at the end of their articles. This one is in the middle, and that is the difference.
- NOTE: "Karlin, our founder." The voice switches to "our." Fine if the article is in the company's voice. It also never says there is something to go read.

### Across all five

- **L10. COMPREHENSION. NOTE.** "the viral "13.7 years younger" gaming study" and "isn't in the paper" appear in all five, nearly word for word. If I read two or three articles on this site in one sitting, which is what the links are asking me to do, I will see the same sentence again and again.
- **L11. TRUST. NOTE.** Links (2) and (5) both lean on "the most-liked reply." Popular is not the same as right. Once is a nice detail. Twice starts to look like the company's favorite quote.

## PART 3. The view-count source line

"View counts: original post 17.2M as read October 11, 2026; the two largest quote-posts 5.8M and 7.9M, and about 33M across eight posts, as read October 6, 2026."

**Confusing.** I can tell the writer is being careful, and that helps trust. But I could not make the numbers add up on first read.

**S1. COMPREHENSION. SHOULD FIX. Two different dates, and the later one comes first.**
The original post is counted on October 11. Everything else is counted on October 6. So is the 33 million out of date? Does the 33 million use the October 11 figure for the original or an older, smaller one? I cannot tell, so I cannot check the total.

**S2. COMPREHENSION. SHOULD FIX. "about 33M across eight posts."**
Does "eight posts" include the original? 17.2 plus 5.8 plus 7.9 is 30.9, so the other posts must make up the rest, but I had to do that sum myself and guess at what was in it. Also "the two largest" are listed smaller one first, which made me double check I had read it right.

**S3. COMPREHENSION. NOTE. "as read."**
I would expect "as of." "As read" made me pause to work out that it means "when I looked."

**S4. TRUST. NOTE. "in its first week."**
Paragraph 2 says the post "passed 30 million views in its first week." The source line gives dates the counts were read but not the date of the post. So I cannot tell whether October 6 is inside that first week.

**Does the subhead square with it?** **No.** See F1. The subhead says a doctor posted one line and more than 30 million people saw it. The source line says that post has 17.2 million views. The 30 million is a total across other people's posts, and it is views, not people. Paragraph 2 is honest about this. The subhead is not.
