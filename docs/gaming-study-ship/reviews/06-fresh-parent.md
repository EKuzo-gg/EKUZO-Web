# 06. Fresh parent read (phone, 375 wide)

Who I am for this read: parent of a twelve-year-old who games a lot. My brother-in-law sent the "13.7 years younger" post last month and my son has quoted it at me since. I have never heard of this company. Five minutes, on my phone, suspicious of anything that asks for my email.

What I looked at: only the screenshots (popup, top, hero, opening, the full page in slices, sticky bar, keep reading). The Instagram video in the middle did not load in the capture, so I only read its caption.

## What did the thing that popped up want, and what did I do?

Before I had read one word of the article, the whole screen was covered by a photo of a kid on a couch and "THEY GAME ~~TOO MUCH.~~ WITH A COACH." Under it: "SEASON 01 · WAITLIST OPEN", a line about a real coach and a team, "Join the list and you'll hear first when sign-ups open", an email box and a red "Save their spot" button.

It wanted my email for a waitlist for something I could not yet name. I hit the X in the corner (easy to find, to be fair) and my guard went up. My thought was "so this is an ad", and I read the first paragraphs looking for the catch.

## What is the truth about "13.7 years younger", and where did I learn it?

The study is real, but it tested 923 adults with an average age of 55, it cannot say gaming caused the better scores, and the "13.7 years younger" figure is not in the paper at all; it came from a 2024 university press release that counted "gamer" differently. I learned that in the very first paragraph under the author's name (the bold "Short answer"), and the yellow "not in the paper" sticker on the picture above it had already tipped me off.

One wobble: much further down (about 60 percent of the way, the "Dubious." bullet) he says that if you do the math yourself you get "about 15 years at the sample's average age of 55, about 12 at 70, and over 20 at 40". So I came away sure the number is not the paper's, but less sure whether it is wrong. See problem 2.

## What does it mean for MY twelve-year-old?

Nothing, directly. The sentence that told me, first paragraph:

> "It can't say the gaming caused that, and no one under 18 was in it, so it tells you nothing about your twelve-year-old."

He repeats it later in the questions section: "This study can't answer that; no one under 18 was in it." What he offers instead is his opinion, labelled as opinion: "I think the better question for a kid is what kind of gaming, and who he plays with."

## Who wrote this, why believe him, and did he sell to me?

Karlin Oei, "Founder of EKUZO". A former shy kid who found his feet in online games, played on the UT Dallas team, and says competing earned him "more than $80,000 in scholarships". He now runs a company that coaches kids.

Why I mostly believe him:
- He has every reason to cheer for this study and he knocks it down instead. "a study of 55-year-olds proves nothing about me or your kid."
- He says "Not my specialty. My read, checked against the paper." and links the paper, the press release, and his own math.
- He has a "Where I stop" section that says a small share of heavy players get into real trouble and "A post that makes you feel better about the hours is not a reason to stop counting them." A salesman would not write that.
- He separates his opinion from the finding: "That's my design choice, not a finding of this study."

Did I feel sold to? Yes, but mostly by the page around him, not by him.
- The popup, before anything else.
- A red "Join Season 01" bar stuck to the bottom of my screen the whole way down.
- In the article: "My answer to the first one is how I run a season", then a black and yellow "SAME GAME. TWO DIFFERENT HOURS." graphic with the company name on it. That is where the article turns into the pitch. It was a soft turn and he had earned it by then, but I noticed.
- "kids in my sessions do pushups and drink water before ranked" and "the reason my sessions have a schedule a kid can see." Small plugs.
- The end: "my coaches answer parent questions", then a big red "Season 01 starts in 2027. Get them on the list." banner, then a newsletter box. Counting the popup and the bar, that is four asks for my details on one article.

## What is this company, and what is "Season 01"?

From the page alone: EKUZO coaches kids in video games (League of Legends is the one named) the way a sports club would, with a coach on the call, the same teammates every week, and sessions with a set end time. The footer lists "EKUZO 100", "EKUZO Teams" and "EKUZO Camps", so there seem to be a few programs. "Season 01" looks like their first season of the team program, starting some time in 2027, and right now all you can do is put your email on a waitlist.

What I could not tell: how old the kids are, whether it is online or in a building, what it costs, how long a season is, or whether "Season 01" means the company has not actually run this yet. That last one nags, because he also talks about "every intake call", "kids in my sessions" and a parent who emailed him in June.

## One thing I would tell my spouse tonight

"That brain study was on 55-year-olds, not kids, and the 13.7 number is not even in it. The guy who wrote this runs a gaming coaching company and even he says it tells us nothing about our son. His point is that what he plays and who he plays with matters as much as the hours."

## What I would say to my son

"I read up on your study. It was 923 grown-ups, average age 55, and nobody under 18. The 13.7 number came from a press release, not the study. So it is not about you. But I will make you a deal: tell me which game, who you are playing with, and when you are stopping before you start, and we can stop fighting about it at the door."

(That last part I took from "Set the end before the start, and make the ending part of the activity instead of an interruption to it." Most useful line in the piece for me.)

## What confused me, felt repeated, felt like filler, or made me stop

1. The camp counting, about a third of the way down under "What a thousand replies said". I had to read it three times.
   - "Four in ten were pro. One in six: 'the study's fine, but.' Under one in ten against. The rest: questions, jokes, noise. Three camps, and the words they use. (A fourth, the skeptics...)"
   - Then the three camps are "one in six", "one in ten" and "one in twelve". "One in six" is used for two different groups two lines apart. I could not tell if the three camps sit inside the "four in ten" or are something else.
   - Then the box right under it is titled "Four camps, in their own words", but the four cards are the top reply plus the three camps. The skeptics, who he just called the fourth, are not in it.
2. "Dubious." bullet (about 60 percent down). The top of the page says the number is "not in the paper". Here he says his own math gives "about 15 years" at 55. So is 13.7 too high, too low, or about right? My son would pounce on "about 15". I think he means "it is not one number and nobody should quote it as one", but he never says that in plain words. "over 20 at 40, where the study runs out of data" lost me completely.
3. A bullet labelled "Needs explanation." that then does not explain: "Cross-sectional and self-reported", "Creyos", "CIHR". The first half sentence after it is clear ("it can't say whether gaming builds the skill or skilled people game more") and is all I needed.
4. Words I did not know and nobody told me: "mid lane", "ranked", "randoms", "public queue", "inhibition", "mood screeners", "the AAP's five Cs". The five Cs one annoyed me most because "How many hours is the right amount?" is my actual question and the answer ends by pointing at something it does not name.
5. Short, clipped lines that I had to guess at: "That's not the plan for your kid; the scholarships were the receipt." and "One person." and "Hard to convince." and "Associated with, every time."
6. "But I run a company that coaches kids in one" (in one what? I worked out "a game" on the second pass).
7. Repeats. "The kind of gaming probably matters as much as the hours" appears three times (quote block, card, questions). The 923 adults and age 55 appear in the short answer and again in the bullets. The nine and ten year old study appears in the bullets and again in the questions. "Set the end before it starts" appears in the paragraph, the graphic, and the questions. Each one is fine alone; together the bottom third felt like I was rereading.
8. The section "The parents who didn't get an answer" says 120 parents asked "which games, what age, how many hours, is it better than sports" and "Not one got an answer." By the end of the article I had not really got one either, except for getting him off the game. Honest, but I felt it.
9. The kids study he leans on "was retracted and replaced in 2023". He is upfront about it, but "retracted" made me pause on whether to trust that paragraph.
10. "Why 'duh'": his life story comes before the study breakdown. I liked it, but with five minutes I would have quit after the short answer if I had not been asked to keep going. The short answer is what saved the article for me.
11. The sixth-grade essay story: "I won't tell you what that proves. I read it twice." Then the caption under the video says "Karlin on the email that made him cry", in the third person, about himself. Slight jolt.
12. The Sources paragraph about view counts ("17.2M as read October 11... 5.8M and 7.9M, and about 33M across eight posts, as read October 6") is a wall of numbers. I skipped it. It did make me think he is careful.

Where I would have stopped if nobody asked me to continue: after the short answer (I had what I came for), or at the camp counting.

## Anything broken or hard to read on the phone

- The red "Join Season 01" bar sits on top of the bottom line or two of text on every screen. In the screenshot it half covers "game three or more hours a week scored".
- The title runs over a grey brush stroke; the "g" in "gaming" sits right on it. Readable, a bit messy.
- The hero picture is cropped so the tweet and the abstract are cut off on the right, and a man's face covers a corner. I did not know who he was until the byline below.
- Byline: "Founder of EKUZO ·" ends its line with a dangling dot, then "Updated October 12, 2026" wraps under it.
- In the reply cards the coloured labels are placed inconsistently: most sit on their own line pushed right, "@bewatchful" has it next to the name.
- "Reply on X" on each card is pale grey and I could not tell if it was a link.
- Big empty stretch where the video should be (I was told to ignore it) and another large blank area between the red banner and "Find us online".
- Red links are easy to see. Text size and spacing were comfortable. No sideways scrolling.

## Would I forward it? Would I click anything?

Forward: yes, to my brother-in-law, with "read the first paragraph." Probably to my spouse too. I would not send it to my son; it is written for me, not him.

Click: maybe "one mom's account of banning it and what came next", since the Fortnite fight is our house. Maybe "my coaches answer parent questions", but only if it did not ask for my email first. I would not tap "Join Season 01" or give the popup my email tonight; I do not know what it is, what it costs, or how old the kids are.
